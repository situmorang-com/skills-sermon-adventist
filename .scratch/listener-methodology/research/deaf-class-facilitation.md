# Facilitating discussion in a deaf, signed Sabbath School class

Research output for issue `01-research-deaf-class-facilitation.md`. Researched 2026-09-04.

## How to read this

Every finding below carries one of three labels:

| Label | Means |
|---|---|
| **[EVIDENCED]** | Peer-reviewed research with a method and a sample. The sample is named, because most of it is children or interpreted mainstream classrooms, not signing adults in a church. |
| **[DENOMINATIONAL]** | Published by the Seventh-day Adventist Church — Adventist Deaf Ministries, Three Angels Deaf Ministries, *Ministry*, or the General Conference Sabbath School Department. Published, not necessarily tested. |
| **[PRACTITIONER]** | One person's account or a teaching-support office's tip sheet. Useful, unverified. |

And where nothing exists, it says **NOT DOCUMENTED** and stops there.

**The largest single finding of this research: the Adventist church has published almost nothing on how to *run* a deaf class.** It has published a great deal on why to have one, how to befriend deaf members, and how to simplify the printed lesson — that last one thoroughly and well. On the mechanics of a discussion in the room, the denominational literature is silent. Everything below on turn-taking, silence and small groups comes from general deaf-education and sign-linguistics research, and most of that research was done on children or on interpreted mainstream classrooms rather than on adults signing directly to each other.

**A standing caveat on transfer.** Almost all the research is on ASL, NGT (Sign Language of the Netherlands), BSL and Libras. None of it is on Bisindo. Turn-taking and gaze findings replicate across every signed language examined so far, which makes transfer reasonable; it is still transfer, and should be said out loud rather than assumed.

## 1. Turn-taking

### The floor is handed by gaze, and only by gaze

**[EVIDENCED]** In teacher-led signed group interaction, Mather (1987) documented three distinct teacher gaze signals that do the work of turn allocation:

- **Group-indicating gaze** — a "smooth arc-like" glance sweeping the group. Signals the question is for everyone.
- **Audience gaze** — the teacher fixes on a midpoint of the group rather than sweeping it. Also signals "all of you," with less motion.
- **Individual gaze** — directed at one person, and it tells *the rest of the group that it is not their turn*: "the floor is to be held by the specific addressee."

Summarised and quoted in Singleton & Crume (2022), *Frontiers in Psychology* 13:934649, from Mather's classroom study. Singleton & Crume add: "students in signing classrooms may use the teacher's eye gaze cues to learn when they are being addressed and whether it is appropriate to make a bid for the teacher's attention."

**What this replaces.** "Go around the circle" is a *spoken*-classroom device: it works because a speaker can be heard without being watched, so the sequence can be inferred from position. In a signed room the equivalent instrument is the teacher's gaze, and it already exists — it is not a workaround, it is the language's own turn-allocation system. The consequence for the guide is that **the teacher's eyes are the microphone**, and every gaze is read as an allocation whether or not the teacher meant it that way. Looking at one person while asking a general question tells the other eleven to stand down.

The sample caveat is real: Mather's classrooms and Singleton & Crume's observations are of deaf *children*. That the same three gaze types govern an adult Sabbath School class is a reasonable inference from the linguistics below, not a documented finding.

### Getting the floor: waving, tapping, and the teacher's refusal

**[EVIDENCED]** Horton & Singleton (2022), *Frontiers in Psychology* 13:935342, review the turn-taking literature across signed languages. Signers bid for a turn with waving, pointing/indexing, tapping, table-thumping, and explicit signs such as LOOK-AT-ME. "Waves are a very common strategy in adult signing conversations."

**[EVIDENCED]** The reason the bid is necessary at all: "the DHH child cannot see a signed turn if they are not looking at the signer" (Horton & Singleton 2022). Lieberman (2015), cited there, found signers "very rarely proceeded with a turn if they did not have the visual attention of their conversational partner." A signed contribution made to a room that is not looking has not been made.

**[EVIDENCED]** Teachers also actively *refuse* the floor, and it is a recognised category of behaviour, not a lapse: "refusing to grant eye contact to student(s) who are deliberately waving or physically touching her" (Singleton & Crume 2022, coded as *Refusal*). Alongside it they code *Delay* actions — WAIT, HOLD, WILL++ — which are the signed equivalent of "hold that thought." This bears directly on the guide's dominant-talker problem: **in a signed room, withholding eye contact is the polite, in-language way to not take a bid**, and it is quieter than any verbal redirect the guide currently prescribes. Note again that this is observed with children; using it on an adult in church is a different social act, and I found nothing that tests it.

**[DENOMINATIONAL]** Evans & Jordan, "The last to 'hear'," *Ministry*, August 2016, give the church's own short list. Relevant items: "To get a deaf person's attention, tap his or her shoulder lightly"; "Maintain eye contact"; "Do not stomp your feet or make huge waving or rude gestures to get the deaf person's attention"; "Do not allow your attention to be diverted by another hearing person." That last one is a real facilitation instruction: a teacher who breaks gaze mid-turn has dropped the class.

### Overlap is not disorder

This is the finding most likely to be misread by a hearing teacher.

**[EVIDENCED]** Coates & Sutton-Spence (2001), *Journal of Sociolinguistics* 5(4):507–529, studied two Deaf friendship groups (one all-female, one all-male) and asked whether Deaf interactants orient to a one-at-a-time model or to a collaborative one. Their contribution was evidence for **the orderliness of overlapping signing** — overlap as collaborative floor, not as interruption. (I could reach the abstract and multiple citing papers, not the full text; the finding as stated is consistent across everything that cites it.)

**[EVIDENCED]** Lepeut & Shaw (2024), *Sign Language Studies* 24(3):722–763, analysed a 75-minute conversation among four ASL signers and found **33 instances of cooperative overlap**. The overlaps did specific jobs — seeking clarification, confirming, and jointly depicting something — and were achieved through lexical repetition and coordinated use of articulators. Their conclusion is that the apparent mess is strategic.

**[EVIDENCED]** Against that, Girard-Groeber (2015) and McCleary & de Arantes Leite (2013), both summarised in Horton & Singleton (2022), argue signers still orient to "minimal gap, minimal overlap," with overlaps occurring at predictable moments. The field has not settled this.

**What the teacher can take.** Two hands moving at once is not necessarily two people fighting for the floor, and the hearing reflex — "one at a time, please" — may suppress the class's own way of confirming and clarifying. But the guide should not swing the other way and prescribe overlap either; the literature is genuinely divided, and the local class's norm is a matter for the teacher to observe, not for a generator to assert.

## 2. Silence

### The four-to-six-second instruction has no signed-language evidence behind it — and one piece of evidence against reading it the same way

**[EVIDENCED]** de Vos, Torreira & Levinson (2015), *Frontiers in Psychology* 6:268, measured 190 question–answer sequences from 16 NGT signers. Measured stroke-to-stroke (i.e. counting only the lexically specified movement, excluding the hand travelling into position):

- mean gap **307 ms**, median **269 ms**
- 29.8% of responses overlapped the question
- 58.3% of responses had a gap over 120 ms

Horton & Singleton (2022) set that beside spoken-language figures: spoken average 208 ms, Japanese 7 ms, Danish 469 ms. NGT at 307 ms sits inside the spoken range. The authors' conclusion is that turn-taking is "a shared cognitive infrastructure underlying all modern human languages."

**What this does and does not settle.** It settles that **a signed conversational gap is as marked as a spoken one.** Signers are not slower at turn transition; a signer who is given a real question and does not answer for five seconds is doing what a hearing person doing the same is doing — thinking, or deciding whether to be honest. The guide's instinct that silence is the question working is not undermined.

It does *not* settle wait time. de Vos et al. measured dyadic and triadic conversation, not a teacher's question to a class, and Rowe's optimal 3–5 seconds (Rowe 1974, 1986) is from hearing classrooms exclusively.

**NOT DOCUMENTED: wait time after a teacher's question in a signed classroom.** I searched for it directly and found nothing. There is no signed-classroom analogue of Rowe. Any number the guide prints for a deaf class — four seconds, six, ten — is borrowed from hearing research, and the guide should say so rather than print it as though it had been measured.

### But visible stillness is not the same object as audible silence

Two things make the *management* of the pause different, both evidenced:

**[EVIDENCED]** Silence in a hearing room is ambient — it costs nothing to sit in, and the teacher can look away, look at notes, look at the floor. In a signed room the teacher's gaze during the pause is still transmitting: individual gaze holds the floor for one person, group-indicating gaze offers it to everyone (Mather, in Singleton & Crume 2022). **There is no neutral place to put your eyes.** Where a hearing teacher waits by doing nothing, a signed teacher waits by holding a deliberate group-indicating or audience gaze, and looking away ends the question rather than extending it. This is an inference from the gaze taxonomy, clearly reasoned, not a finding anyone has tested.

**[DENOMINATIONAL/PRACTITIONER]** Doss, *Can You Hear Us?: A Quick Guide to Deaf Ministry* (Three Angels Deaf Ministries, 2014), p. 24: "You may notice that Deaf individuals may not pay attention and stare at the interpreter at all times. That does not mean that they are not interested in what you have to say. They are just giving their eyes a break. (Keep in mind that having to pay attention to one spot for a period of time is very tiring for the eyes.)" So a break in gaze during a pause may be rest, not disengagement — a hearing teacher will misread it as the question failing and rescue the silence early.

## 3. Attention: what else is single-channel

The charting session's conclusion — text and signing are sequential — is correct, and it is the best-evidenced item in this whole file. It also generalises further than the screen.

**[EVIDENCED]** Marschark et al., "Classroom Interpreting and Visual Information Processing in Mainstream Education for Deaf Students: Live or Memorex®?", *Journal of Deaf Studies and Deaf Education* (2006; PMC1440927). Eye-tracking of 32 college students during lectures — 10 hearing, 11 skilled deaf signers, 11 new deaf signers. Total gaze distribution:

| | Instructor | Display | Interpreter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hearing | 62% | 34% | 4% |
| Deaf (combined) | 16% | 22% | 63% |

The authors found **no evidence of successful divided attention** between sources: deaf students switched sequentially rather than processing concurrently, and the sequential presentation of verbal and visual material increases cognitive load.

Caveat, and it matters: that study is *interpreted* instruction, which is explicitly not the Efata Tuli situation. What transfers is not the interpreter finding but the underlying one — **the visual channel admits one source at a time, and the cost is paid in switching, not in bandwidth.** That constraint is identical whether the signer is an interpreter or the teacher.

**[EVIDENCED]** Same principle, stated at its root by Singleton & Crume (2022): "if they are not looking at the language model, they are not getting linguistic input."

### So the following are all one channel, and compete with signing

Each of these is a direct application of the finding above, not a separately evidenced claim:

- **The screen.** Established already. Text and signing are sequential.
- **Any printed handout.** Same object as the screen.
- **Note-taking.** A member who is writing is not receiving. This is the classic consequence of the Marschark finding.
- **Another member's turn.** If a second person signs while the class is watching the first, one of them is unreceived — this is precisely the overlap question in §1.
- **The teacher's own gaze.** Consuming it is how the class knows who has the floor (§1), so it is not free either.

**Practical consequence, evidenced:** everything the class must *read* has to be given its own slot with no signing over it, and the return from screen to signer has to be signalled and waited for. The guide's timed plan should contain read-slots and sign-slots, not merged ones.

**NOT DOCUMENTED:** how long a read-slot should be for a congregation reading Terjemahan Baru off a screen. That depends on the class's Indonesian reading rate, which nobody has measured for Indonesian deaf adults (see §5).

## 4. Small groups of three

**NOT DOCUMENTED.** This is the ticket's cleanest gap. I found no research, Adventist or general, on whether breaking a signed class into triads works, fragments sightlines, or does anything else. What exists is adjacent and weaker:

**[EVIDENCED, adjacent]** Lepeut & Shaw (2024) show that a four-signer group sustains a coherent multiparty conversation for 75 minutes with cooperative overlap as a working feature. That is evidence that small signed groups *function* — it is not evidence about splitting a class into them, and the group was friends conversing, not a Bible study.

**[EVIDENCED, adjacent]** The visual-channel constraint (§3) means that a triad has a hard geometric requirement a hearing triad does not: all three must see all three, simultaneously and continuously. Three chairs in a row does not satisfy it; three chairs in a triangle does. This follows from the evidence, but nobody has published it as an instruction.

**[PRACTITIONER]** Gallaudet's DeafSpace programme documents that when Deaf people gather they rearrange furniture into a "conversation circle" for clear sightlines, and adjust shades and lighting first — reported by the National Endowment for the Arts and in Gallaudet's own campus-design material. Note this is *self-organised* behaviour being documented, which is a good sign: a deaf class asked to form triads will very likely arrange itself correctly without being told how.

**[PRACTITIONER]** Teaching-support tip sheets (Start ASL; several university disability-services pages) recommend semicircle or circle seating so all participants see each other, and groups of four or five. These are tip sheets citing no study. I record them as the state of the advice, not as findings.

**Honest position for the guide.** Whether triads solve the dominant talker in a signed room is unknown. What *is* known is that the dominant-talker problem has an in-language solution the hearing guide does not know about — the teacher declining eye contact to a bid (§1) — and that solution is documented where the triad solution is not. That may be the better move to carry into the deaf profile, with the triad offered only as something the teacher may already do and can keep doing.

## 5. Simplified Indonesian register

Here the denomination has done real, checkable work — in English.

### The church's own worked example: the Easy Reading edition

**[DENOMINATIONAL]** The *Easy Reading Edition* of the Adult Sabbath School Bible Study Guide is produced by Three Angels Deaf Ministries "working closely with the GC Sabbath School Department" (Doss 2014, p. 32), and Adventist Deaf Ministries lists it as serving "the spiritual needs of the Adventist Deaf community," noting the weekly format "includes helpful definitions of certain key words along with illustrations of important ideas" (adventistdeaf.org, Deaf Resources). ADM adds that it "uses words more commonly used in sign language."

It is fetchable. The Adventech API that the skill already uses serves it as quarterly id `<YYYY-MM>-er` alongside the standard `<YYYY-MM>`:

```bash
curl -s https://sabbath-school.adventech.io/api/v2/en/quarterlies/2026-03-er/lessons/01/days/03/read/index.json
```

I pulled the same day in both editions (Q3 2026, lesson 1, day 3, "From Athens to Corinth") and measured:

| | Standard | Easy Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Words in the day | 1,079 | 401 |
| Sentences | 76 | 34 |
| Mean words per sentence | 14.2 | 11.8 |
| Longest sentence | 53 words | 25 words |

The mean barely moves. **The register control is almost entirely in the longest sentences, the vocabulary, and the ordering** — not in a uniform shortening. Comparing the two texts directly, the transformations are:

1. **The question comes before the reference, not after.** Standard: "Read Acts 17:16–34. Where was Paul before going to Corinth…?" Easy: "Where was Paul before he went to Corinth? … Read Acts 17:16–34 for the answers." The reader is told what to look for before being sent to look.
2. **One clause per sentence.** "Paul left Berea because Jews came to Berea and caused trouble for Paul" replaces "went there with the help of some friends because of the opposition that he faced at Berea."
3. **The name repeats where the standard uses a pronoun.** "Paul … Paul … Paul," not "he … he." Anaphora across sentences is what gets dropped.
4. **Religious and Latinate vocabulary is replaced, not defined:** *synagogue* → "the Jewish church"; *missionary journey* → "preaching trip"; *apostles* → "His followers."
5. **Proper nouns that cannot be replaced are glossed in-line:** "The Areopagus is the Greek court in Athens."
6. **Idiom is removed.** "took advantage of every opportunity to do it" becomes "Paul talked about Jesus everywhere." "was occupied with the word" becomes "spent all his time preaching."
7. **The Bible translation changes with the register.** The standard day quotes ESV and NKJV; the Easy Reading day quotes WE (Worldwide English), NLV, ERV and EASY. The simplification extends to the quoted Scripture itself.
8. **The closing application shortens and concretises.** Standard: "What can we learn from Paul's missionary activity…? What makes sharing the gospel so challenging in our cities…?" Easy: "Think about the places you go every day. Who do you meet in those places?"

Point 7 is the one with a direct bearing on the skill's Bible-text policy, and it is the one that does not port: **there is no Indonesian Easy Reading edition.** The Adventech API's `in` (Bahasa Indonesia) quarterly list carries only the standard ids — no `-er` variant for any quarter back to 2019. So for an Indonesian deaf class the teacher is transposing the eight principles above onto Terjemahan Baru by hand, and TB has no simplified counterpart in the way English has WE or ERV. That is a real, unsolved gap, and the guide should not pretend otherwise.

### Reading level: what is known, and how weakly it applies here

**[EVIDENCED, US]** Traxler (2000), *Journal of Deaf Studies and Deaf Education* 5(4):337–348 — the Stanford Achievement Test 9th edition deaf norming — is the source of the widely repeated figure that about half of deaf and hard-of-hearing readers aged 18 and over read at or below a fourth-grade level. Traxler herself cautions that her participants were not nationally representative, a caution routinely dropped by those who cite her.

**[DENOMINATIONAL]** Blake, "Improved ministry for the deaf," *Ministry*, December 2001, states the same thing in the church's own words: "Because their first language is sign language, the average North American deaf person reads at a fourth- or fifth-grade level. This means that the church's printed materials usually present barriers for the deaf."

**[PRACTITIONER/SECONDARY, Indonesia]** The University of Melbourne's *Indonesia at Melbourne* reports that Indonesian deaf students aged 17–18 write at the level of hearing 9–10 year-olds and have limited reading vocabulary and difficulty with grammar. **I could not load this page** — the server refused connection repeatedly — and I have it only through search summaries. **Treat the Indonesian figure as unverified until the page is read.** Chasing it to its underlying study is unfinished work.

### The language politics matter, and they are not decoration

**[EVIDENCED/SECONDARY]** Indonesia has two systems. **Bisindo** is the natural sign language that emerged in deaf social networks from at least the 1950s, with its own grammar. **SIBI** is an artificial standardised system that manually codes spoken Indonesian grammar and vocabulary, and it is what government policy has historically mandated in formal deaf education. For many deaf students, learning through SIBI is comparable to learning in a foreign tongue. Palfreyman's thesis (*Sign language varieties of Indonesia*, University of Central Lancashire) and *Variation in Indonesian Sign Language* (De Gruyter) are the primary linguistic sources; I read the summary literature and the search-level reporting, not the full thesis.

**Consequence for register.** "Simplified Indonesian" is not simply "shorter Indonesian." Written Indonesian is a **second language** to a Bisindo signer, related to their first language roughly as written English is to an ASL signer. So:

- Sentence length matters less than the eight transformations above — particularly the pronoun rule (3), the idiom rule (6), and the gloss rule (5).
- **Adventist in-group vocabulary is the highest-risk category**, because it is simultaneously essential and untranslatable-by-shortening: *bait suci*, *pengadilan pemeriksaan*, *Roh Nubuat*. The Easy Reading edition's answer is to gloss in-line at first use, and that is a portable instruction.
- The existing `teaching-methods.md` guidance for mixed-language classes — "Adventist terms often stay Indonesian even in an English sentence… That is normal register, not an error" — is about hearing bilinguals and does not transfer. For a deaf class the term needs a gloss, not a pass.

**[DENOMINATIONAL] One dated claim to reject.** Blake (2001) writes that concepts sometimes need explaining "to people who, because of their handicap, may find it challenging to think in the abstract." That is a claim about cognition, it is not supported by anything in the current literature, and the same denomination's later material contradicts its spirit: Evans & Jordan (2016) instruct "Do not treat the deaf adult like a child," and Blake's own article says elsewhere "their minds work very well. Treat them as equals." Register control is about **language access**, not about capacity. The guide should carry the simplification and drop the rationale.

### One denominational statement that endorses the discussion format itself

**[DENOMINATIONAL]** Doss (2014), p. 15, on why a Deaf church differs from an interpreted one: "Deaf people best learn interactively. In a Deaf church, they are free to ask questions, seek clarification, and discuss meaning together." This is the closest thing the church has published to a rationale for running a deaf Sabbath School as a discussion rather than a lecture, and it is worth citing in the guide because it comes from inside the tradition.

## What I could not find

Listed plainly, because the ticket asks for it and because these are the places a plausible-sounding invention would slot in most easily.

1. **Any Adventist material on facilitating a deaf class.** ADM, Possibility Ministries, 3ADM, *Ministry* and the NAD Deaf Ministries department publish on befriending deaf members, interpreting, resources, and the Easy Reading edition. On turn-taking, questioning, wait time, or group work in a deaf Sabbath School — nothing.
2. **Wait time in a signed classroom.** No signed analogue of Rowe exists that I can find. The four-to-six-second figure is hearing-classroom research.
3. **Small groups in a signed class.** No study. Only adjacent evidence (a functioning four-signer group) and unsourced tip sheets.
4. **Any research on Bisindo interaction.** Every turn-taking and gaze finding above is ASL, NGT, BSL or Libras.
5. **An Indonesian Easy Reading quarterly.** Confirmed absent from the Adventech API for every Indonesian quarter from 2019 to 2026-03.
6. **A simplified Indonesian Bible comparable to WE/ERV/EASY.** Not found; not searched exhaustively, and worth a separate look, because points 4–7 of the Easy Reading method depend on one existing.
7. **Efata Tuli, or any documented Indonesian Adventist deaf congregation.** Nothing in Adventist Review, the Adventist Encyclopedia's Jakarta Conference entry, adventist.or.id, or the ADM division-contacts list. The class this guide serves is undocumented, which means the teacher's own observation outranks everything in this file.
8. **Full text of three sources I cite at second hand:** Coates & Sutton-Spence (2001) — abstract and citing papers only; Mather (1987) — through Singleton & Crume's summary and direct quotations only; the Indonesian literacy figure — search summaries only, page unreachable.
9. **The Jakarta special-schools teacher-strategies study** (Cogent Education, 2023, doi 10.1080/2331186X.2023.2258294) — the single most locally relevant paper found, on deaf teaching strategies in Jakarta. Blocked by the publisher at every attempt. **Worth one more try from a browser**; it may answer parts of §1 and §5 for the actual context.
