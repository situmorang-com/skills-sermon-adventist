# Bible Study: 1 Corinthians 12–14 — Spiritual Gifts and the More Excellent Way

*Prepared for: Sabbath School / small-group teaching (Lesson 06, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Q3 2026)*
*Translation: KJV (English), with Terjemahan Baru (TB) comparison in the word studies*
*Source study: derived from the Deep Made Simple video walkthrough of Lesson 06 ([transcript](transcript.md)), then independently researched against SDABC, Adventist scholarship, Ellen G. White, and Matthew Henry*

## 1. Passage Context

**Book overview.** First Corinthians is a letter from Paul to a congregation he founded around A.D. 51 during his eighteen months in the city (Acts 18:1–11), written from Ephesus roughly A.D. 55. Corinth was a wealthy Roman colony and port, and the church there was a mix of Greeks, Romans, and Jews, most of them recent converts out of paganism. Paul writes because he has heard reports and received questions, and almost every chapter is Paul putting out a fire.

**Genre.** Occasional epistle. Pastoral correction, not systematic theology. That matters: Paul is answering a specific church's specific misbehavior, and the shape of his argument is set by the shape of their problem.

**Placement.** Paul has worked through faction-fighting over favorite preachers (chs. 1–4), tolerated immorality (ch. 5), lawsuits between members (ch. 6), marriage and singleness (ch. 7), meat offered to idols (chs. 8–10), and disorder at the Lord's Supper (ch. 11). Chapters 12–14 are the next item on the list: what happens when this church actually gathers to worship. SDABC marks the seam plainly — this chapter begins a new subject that runs through chapter 14, and the subject is spiritual gifts, about which there was both misunderstanding and outright abuse, including rivalry between the possessors of different gifts (6SDABC on 12:1). Chapter 15 then turns to the resurrection. So 12–14 is one continuous argument, and chapter 13 is not a detour inside it. It is the hinge.

**Structure of the argument:**

| Section | Paul's move |
|---|---|
| 12:1–11 | The gifts are *grace*, given by the one Spirit as He wills |
| 12:12–31 | The church is a *body*: diverse, interdependent, no member expendable |
| 12:31b–13:13 | Love is the *more excellent way* that must run through every gift |
| 14:1–40 | Therefore prefer what *edifies*: intelligible speech over unintelligible |

**The passage** (representative core verses; read all three chapters aloud if time allows):

> "Now there are diversities of gifts, but the same Spirit. And there are differences of administrations, but the same Lord. And there are diversities of operations, but it is the same God which worketh all in all. But the manifestation of the Spirit is given to every man to profit withal." (1 Corinthians 12:4–7, KJV)

> "For as the body is one, and hath many members, and all the members of that one body, being many, are one body: so also is Christ. ... Nay, much more those members of the body, which seem to be more feeble, are necessary." (1 Corinthians 12:12, 22, KJV)

> "And though I bestow all my goods to feed the poor, and though I give my body to be burned, and have not charity, it profiteth me nothing." (1 Corinthians 13:3, KJV)

> "Even so ye, forasmuch as ye are zealous of spiritual gifts, seek that ye may excel to the edifying of the church." (1 Corinthians 14:12, KJV)

## 2. Historical and Cultural Background

**A Roman colony with no old money.** Corinth was destroyed by Rome in 146 B.C. and refounded as a Roman colony by Julius Caesar in 44 B.C. By Paul's day it was barely a century old, populated largely by freedmen, veterans, and traders, and it had no entrenched aristocracy. Status was therefore up for grabs, and Corinth was famous for chasing it. That is the social air the church breathed. A congregation of status-anxious people handed a set of visible, impressive, divinely given abilities is a congregation with a predictable problem.

**Pagan religion ran on spectacle.** Most of these believers came out of Greco-Roman cult. In that world the mark of genuine contact with the divine was ecstatic experience: trance, frenzy, unintelligible inspired utterance. The more overwhelming the display, the more credible the deity's presence. That instinct did not evaporate at baptism. It came into church with them, and they applied it to the real gifts of the Spirit, treating them as proof of spiritual rank rather than as tools for service. Paul's diagnosis is the same disease as the rest of the letter — pride and division — in a new arena.

**Three details a modern reader misses:**

1. **"Sounding brass" and "tinkling cymbal" were temple instruments, not orchestra instruments.** Gongs and cymbals were standard equipment in the ecstatic cults of the region, used to build crowd frenzy and get a god's attention. When Paul says a loveless gift is a clanging cymbal, the insult lands harder than we hear it: he is telling former pagans that their showy, loveless worship is not Christian worship at all. It is the noise of the temples they left, relabeled. The cymbal's role in Greco-Roman cult is well attested in classical sources; verify in Keener's IVP background commentary or a classical dictionary before asserting it from the pulpit.

2. **"Jesus is Lord" was a politically dangerous sentence.** The Roman world had another lord, and increasingly the empire used the confession of Caesar as lord as a loyalty test. To say *Jesus is Lord* was by implication to say Caesar is not. SDABC reads 12:3 as Paul handing the church a **rule for testing utterances that claimed to come from the Spirit** (6SDABC on 12:3) — which fits a setting where ecstatic speech was common and its source was not self-evident.

3. **Corinth was multilingual by trade.** It was an international port where a great many languages were actually spoken. That is not incidental background — it is the practical setting in which unintelligible speech in a public meeting became a real communication problem, and it shapes Paul's worry about the outsider who walks in and understands nothing (1 Cor 14:16, 23). This is Timm and Esmond's argument, quoted in Section 4.

## 3. Key Word Studies

### 3.1. Gifts — χαρίσματα (*charismata*)

| Field | |
|---|---|
| English (KJV) | "gifts" (1 Cor 12:4, 9, 28, 30, 31) |
| Indonesian (TB) | "karunia" — *"Ada rupa-rupa karunia, tetapi satu Roh"* (1 Kor 12:4) |
| Greek | χάρισμα, *charisma*, plural *charismata* |
| Strong's | G5486, from χάρις *charis* (G5485), grace |
| Literal / root | A thing freely and graciously given; a gift of grace |
| Range | Used of salvation itself (Rom 6:23), of deliverance from danger (2 Cor 1:11), of celibacy (1 Cor 7:7), and of the ministry gift Timothy received (1 Tim 4:14, 2 Tim 1:6) |
| Translation comparison | KJV, NKJV, ESV "gifts"; NIV "gifts"; NASB "gifts." No English version carries the *grace* root visibly, so the whole argument is invisible in translation |
| Why it matters | SDABC glosses *charismata* as literally "gifts of grace" (6SDABC on 12:4). The word itself refutes the Corinthian error. A wage is earned; a *charisma* is not. The Corinthians were treating their gifts as evidence of superior spirituality, and Paul chose a noun that means the credit belongs entirely to the giver |

**Note on 12:1.** The phrase KJV renders "spiritual gifts" in 12:1 is not *charismata* at all but πνευματικῶν (*pneumatikōn*), "spiritual things" — the word "gifts" is supplied by translators. TB likewise supplies: *"Sekarang tentang karunia-karunia Roh."* Paul's own preferred vocabulary is *charismata*, and he may be picking up the Corinthians' word in 12:1 in order to correct it with his own in 12:4.

**Caution on the *charis* / *chara* link.** *Charis* (grace) and *chara* (joy) do share a root associated with rejoicing, so it is defensible to say the word family carries a note of delight. But this is exactly where the root fallacy tempts a preacher. Paul's *argument* in 12:4–11 turns on **unearnedness**, not on joy. Preach the grace; mention the joy as a grace note, not as the exegesis.

### 3.2. Charity / Love — ἀγάπη (*agapē*)

| Field | |
|---|---|
| English (KJV) | "charity" (1 Cor 13:1–13) |
| Indonesian (TB) | "kasih" — *"Kasih itu sabar; kasih itu murah hati"* (1 Kor 13:4) |
| Greek | ἀγάπη, *agapē* |
| Strong's | G26 |
| Literal / root | Self-giving, deliberate, benevolent love; in NT usage the characteristic love of God |
| Range | God's own nature (1 John 4:8); the command that sums up the law (Rom 13:10); the first fruit of the Spirit (Gal 5:22) |
| Translation comparison | KJV "charity" (via Latin *caritas*); NKJV, ESV, NIV, NASB all "love." **The KJV rendering is now actively misleading** — modern English "charity" means almsgiving, which is precisely one of the things Paul says is worthless *without* agapē in 13:3 |
| Why it matters | Matthew Henry flagged this drift already in 1710: the word does not mean charity in the common sense of alms-giving, but love in its fullest extent, love to God and to fellow-Christians (MHBCC 1076.2). If you teach from the KJV, say "love" out loud or the class will hear the wrong word |

**Structure worth showing the class.** SDABC counts seven positive characteristics of love in vv. 4–7 and eight attitudes foreign to its nature (6SDABC on 13:4), and notes that throughout the paragraph you catch glimpses of the specific faults at Corinth that stood in direct contrast. Nearly every item is a verb, not an adjective. Paul is not describing a feeling. He is describing a practice.

### 3.3. Edifying — οἰκοδομή / οἰκοδομέω (*oikodomē* / *oikodomeō*)

| Field | |
|---|---|
| English (KJV) | "edifying" (1 Cor 14:12), "edifieth" (14:4), "to edification" (14:3) |
| Indonesian (TB) | "membangun" — *"hendaklah kamu berusaha mempergunakannya untuk membangun Jemaat"* (1 Kor 14:12) |
| Greek | οἰκοδομή, from *oikos* (house) + *demō* (to build) |
| Strong's | G3619 (noun), G3618 (verb) |
| Literal / root | To build a house; construction, a building under construction |
| Range | The church as God's building (1 Cor 3:9); building up the body (Eph 4:12, 16); speech that builds rather than tears down (Eph 4:29) |
| Translation comparison | KJV "edifying"; NKJV "edification"; ESV "build up"; NIV "build up the church"; NASB "edification." The modern versions are clearer — "edify" has faded into vague religious vocabulary, while *build* is a construction word |
| Why it matters | This is the controlling word of chapter 14 and Paul's actual test for any gift in public worship. TB's *membangun* keeps the concreteness the English loses. Not *does this look spiritual*, but *is anything getting built* |

### 3.4. Tongue / Language — γλῶσσα (*glōssa*)

| Field | |
|---|---|
| English (KJV) | "tongues" (1 Cor 12:10, 28, 30; 13:1, 8; 14 throughout) |
| Indonesian (TB) | "bahasa roh" (1 Kor 14:2); but "bahasa manusia dan bahasa malaikat" in 13:1 |
| Greek | γλῶσσα, *glōssa* — the physical tongue, or a language |
| Strong's | G1100 |
| Literal / root | Tongue (organ); by extension, a language |
| Range | Physical tongue (Mark 7:33); the languages of the nations (Rev 7:9, 14:6); the Pentecost gift (Acts 2:4–11) |
| Translation comparison | KJV supplies the word "unknown" throughout ch. 14 — "an *unknown* tongue" — and SDABC flags this: the word "unknown" is supplied by the translators (6SDABC on 14:2, 14:27). It is not in the Greek. That single supplied adjective has shaped centuries of interpretation |
| Why it matters | If *glōssa* means language, the gift is a communication miracle, and Paul's whole insistence on interpretation makes obvious sense. TB's *bahasa roh* leans the other way and is worth naming aloud to an Indonesian class as an interpretive choice, not a neutral rendering |

### 3.5. Prophesy — προφητεύω (*prophēteuō*)

| Field | |
|---|---|
| English (KJV) | "prophesieth" (1 Cor 14:3), "prophesy" (14:1, 39) |
| Indonesian (TB) | "bernubuat" — *"siapa yang bernubuat, ia berkata-kata kepada manusia, ia membangun, menasihati dan menghibur"* (1 Kor 14:3) |
| Greek | προφητεύω, from *pro* (before, or on behalf of) + *phēmi* (to speak) |
| Strong's | G4395 |
| Literal / root | To speak forth, to speak for another. Prediction is one possible sense of *pro*, but "on behalf of" is the more common force |
| Range | Foretelling (Matt 11:13); Spirit-given proclamation without prediction (Acts 19:6, 1 Cor 11:4–5); Caiaphas speaking beyond his intent (John 11:51) |
| Translation comparison | All major versions "prophesy." The interpretive weight is not in the translation but in the reader's assumption. Modern readers hear *fortune-telling*; Paul defines it functionally in 14:3 as speech that builds, exhorts, and comforts |
| Why it matters | SDABC describes the prophet as one who stands between God and men and reveals God's will to men (6SDABC on 13:2), and notes the prophet spoke in a language the hearers knew (6SDABC on 14:3). Paul's own definition in 14:3 is the safest fence against both dismissing the gift and inflating it |

## 4. Research and Commentary Insights

### Biblical Research Institute

BRI's position on this passage is definite, and a class that asks about modern tongues-speaking deserves to hear it stated plainly: the gift of tongues in Scripture is the Spirit-given capacity to speak real human languages, not ecstatic or unintelligible utterance, and contemporary glossolalia should not be equated with the New Testament gift. Two BRI articles carry the argument.

**"The gift of tongues in 1 Corinthians 14"** (BRI, 28 February 2012). It sets out the three interpretations on offer: the language of angels, drawn from "tongues of . . . angels" in 13:1; the capacity to speak other languages by the Spirit, as in Acts 2; and ecstatic unintelligible utterance, drawn from "no one understands him" in 14:2. Then it applies a hermeneutical rule that matters more than the conclusion it reaches: a difficult passage is read in the light of passages where the same subject is clear. Acts 2 is clear, and there is general agreement it means foreign languages. Mark 16:17's "they will speak in new tongues" could hardly mean anything else. Outside 1 Corinthians 14, the gift always designates that capacity.

On chapter 14 itself the article makes three observations a teacher can use directly:

1. The gift there does appear unintelligible, which is genuinely different from Acts 2. Some conclude the gift had more than one expression; others harmonize the two by noting that in Corinth the languages were simply unknown to the hearers, which is why translation was needed (14:13).
2. The gift does not involve loss of self-control. Only two or three speak, and without an interpreter they keep silent (14:27, 28). Controllable speech is not ecstatic speech.
3. Paul does not expect or encourage every member to receive it, and in church he seems to discourage it. He treats prophecy as more meaningful, and he never makes tongues an indispensable sign of conversion or of receiving the Spirit.

Its conclusion: the biblical support for reading the gift as languages is very strong, and *glōssa*, when used of the gift, means "language" rather than "tongue."

**"What are the Tongues in 1 Corinthians?"** (BRI, 2010) reaches the same place through the vocabulary. The verb "to speak" occurs 34 times in 1 Corinthians; in chapter 14 it appears 10 times with "tongues" and 14 times without, and every time it appears without, the speaking involves a real language with content that can be communicated. A word keeps its meaning inside a single context unless the writer redefines it, and Paul does not. The article then walks the phrase through Scripture: Job 33:2, Psalm 37:30, Psalm 109:2, Isaiah 28:11's "stammering lips and a foreign tongue," Jeremiah 9:5, Mark 16:17, Acts 2:4 and 2:11, Acts 10:46, Acts 19:6, and 1 Corinthians 12:30 and 13:1. Foreign languages throughout.

Its reading of the disputed verse is worth having ready, because someone will raise it: 14:2 describes a person speaking a foreign language in a setting where nobody understands it, so he speaks only to God, since God understands every language. The gift at Corinth was genuine. It was being misused, and Paul is calling the church back to a use that blesses believers and unbelievers instead of hindering them.

Both articles are on the BRI site: [The gift of tongues in 1 Corinthians 14](https://adventistbiblicalresearch.org/articles/the-gift-of-tongues-in-1-corinthians-14) and [What are the Tongues in 1 Corinthians?](https://adventistbiblicalresearch.org/articles/what-are-the-tongues-in-1-corinthians). The second one's footnotes point to **Gerhard F. Hasel, *Speaking in Tongues: Biblical Speaking in Tongues and Contemporary Glossolalia*** (Adventist Theological Society Publications, 1991), which is the fullest Adventist treatment of the question and the right next step if your class wants to go deeper.

### Andrews Bible Commentary (2020)

Andrews Bible Commentary treats this material in three places: **ABC-NT p. 1614** on the gifts and the body, **p. 1644** on tongues, and **p. 1473** on the Pentecost gift as foreign languages. Those pages are not summarized here. Check ABC-NT on 1 Corinthians 12–14 in your own copy; exact paragraph references are in the ledger.

### Seventh-day Adventist Bible Commentary (SDABC, vol. 6)

This is the richest source available for the passage, and it lands in several places worth teaching. Everything below is drawn from the verbatim text.

**On the unit and the problem (12:1).** SDABC states that the chapter begins a new subject running through chapter 14, that the subject is spiritual gifts, that there was doubtless considerable misunderstanding about them, and that there was clearly some abuse of the gifts as well as unfortunate rivalry between possessors of various gifts. That is the whole diagnosis in one paragraph, and it is a denominational commentary saying it, which is useful when a class member suspects you invented the conflict.

**On sovereignty as the cure for pride (12:11).** All the endowments supplied to the church are imparted by the Holy Spirit working in and through believers; and since God controls their working, they must all be in harmony with His plan for finishing His work on earth. Then the line to underline: **the knowledge that all the gifts come from God should be enough to forbid any display of pride in their possession.** Pride is not merely unkind here. It is a category error about where the gift came from.

**On the body (12:12, 12:21).** The human body is one organism composed of parts each having its own indispensable function, all uniting harmoniously, all under the same guiding power, the head. And on "I have no need of you" — this verse rebukes the pride of those who felt more highly gifted; **the most gifted church members are as much dependent on the less favored as the latter are on the former**; the failure of one part affects the efficiency of all the others; the contribution of the apparently most insignificant office is important for the harmonious development of the entire organization. SDABC's conclusion is blunt: this being the case, there is no place at all for pride *or discontent* in the church. Note both directions. The gifted member's contempt and the ordinary member's self-pity are the same sin from opposite ends.

**On the weaker members (12:22).** SDABC is honestly tentative here: it is not certain which members of the body Paul refers to, though perhaps there is an allusion to parts that appear structurally weaker and need protection. Worth noting, because the popular application (the hidden vital organs — heart, lungs) is a *sermon* reading, not a certain exegetical one. It preaches well. Present it as illustration, not as what Paul demonstrably meant.

**On the point of chapter 13 (13:1).** Having defined the place of the gifts in chapter 12, Paul now shows that possessing all these gifts and other qualities besides **does not make one a Christian** if he lacks the supreme gift of love. SDABC also passes along Harnack's assessment of the chapter as the greatest, strongest, deepest thing Paul ever wrote.

**On the ostentatious almsgiving of 13:3.** SDABC works the Greek *psōmizō*, to feed by putting bits of food into the mouth, to dole out in morsels, and suggests the wealthy customarily doled out alms at their gate, distributing in small portions to many people so a larger number would be ready to praise the donor. If all a man possessed were doled out this way while true love was absent, it would be empty hypocrisy and of no spiritual value. That is a sharper reading than most classes have heard, and it is Adventist.

**On why love outlasts the gifts (13:8, 13:13).** Genuine love does not fall off like a leaf or a flower; in days of stress as well as when all is bright, love remains the same. And SDABC grounds the permanence doctrinally: **love is the very foundation of law, and the law of God is eternal.** On 13:13, "abideth" conveys permanence — apart from love, everything dealt with in the chapter, including prophecy and tongues, will cease to be of value or be canceled, while faith, hope, and love do not pass away.

**On worship order (14:40).** *Kata taxin*, "according to rank," "according to arrangement" — a military term for the orderly array of an army in ranks. Everything should be done decorously, as becomes the worship of the Omnipotent Creator, with no confusion, unnecessary noise, or disorder. Note that SDABC cross-references Habakkuk 2:20 and several EGW passages here (Ev 314, 636, 637; Ed 243; PK 48, 49; PP 303; 4T 626) — a ready-made reading list if you want to press the reverence application.

**One pointer.** SDABC on 14:2 defers the question of the *nature* of tongues to an **Additional Note at the end of chapter 14** (6SDABC), the denomination's fullest classic treatment of the question. That note is not summarized here. If your class will raise modern tongues-speaking, read it first.

### Adventist Scholars

**Alberto Timm and Dwain Esmond, eds., *The Gift of Prophecy* (2015)**, p. 174. This is the most directly useful modern Adventist scholarship on the passage, and it makes four points a teacher can use:

1. **"Edification" (*oikodomēn*) controls the whole of chapter 14** and gives the key to the nature of authentic prophecy.
2. **The chapter is bracketed by a literary inclusio** — "seek to prophesy" in v. 1 and again in v. 39 — inside which Paul sets up a sharp comparison between tongues and prophecy.
3. **Prophecy is not inherently greater than tongues or any other gift**, since all gifts come from the same Spirit and are given for the common good. Prophecy is greater in this context *precisely because it is intelligible and can therefore edify.* This is an important restraint: Paul is not establishing a permanent hierarchy of gifts, he is applying one criterion.
4. **On the nature of tongues:** the volume argues that Acts 2:5–11, where the crowds heard the 120 in various foreign languages, provides the strongest background for 1 Corinthians 12–14; that *glōssa* refers to foreign languages, something obvious in an international commercial community like Corinth where a cacophony of languages must have been spoken; and that unknown foreign languages divide rather than unite, which is why interpretation matters. Speaking a foreign language for its own sake, without interpretation and without regard for the community's participation, was an abuse and was divisive.

The volume's own footnotes point to Gordon Fee's *First Epistle to the Corinthians*, Kenneth Bailey, C. H. Talbert, and the *Dictionary of Paul and His Letters* — a solid next-step bibliography if you want to go deeper than this study.

**Where to go next.** Ángel Rodríguez and the BRI staff are the right voices for the doctrinal question of whether and how the gifts continue. Richard Davidson is the right voice on hermeneutical method if someone wants to argue from Pentecost typology. These are pointers to the right scholars, not summaries of what they have published on this passage. Consult the works before citing them.

### Classic Commentary Voices

**Matthew Henry** on 13:1–3 (*Complete Commentary*, p. 1076). Henry works verse by verse and is worth reading aloud in class for two reasons.

First, his lexical correction, noted in the word study above: agapē is not "charity" in the common sense of almsgiving but love in its fullest extent, growing out of sincere devotion to God.

Second, his aphorisms are teaching gold, and they are genuinely his:

> "It is the charitable heart, not the voluble tongue, that is acceptable with God."

> "A clear and deep head is of no signification, without a benevolent and charitable heart."

> "Moving mountains is a great achievement in the account of men; but one dram of charity is, in God's account, of much greater worth than all the faith of this sort in the world."

Henry also notices something exegetically sharp: Paul specifies tongues *first* in 13:1 precisely because tongues was the gift on which the Corinthians chiefly valued themselves and despised their brethren. And on 13:3 he presses the uncomfortable point that the outward act of giving alms may proceed from a very bad principle — there may be an open and lavish hand where there is no charitable heart.

**Doctrinal flag on Henry:** he assumes the immortality of the soul and eternal conscious punishment. Nothing in 1 Corinthians 12–14 forces that issue, so he is safe to use here. Do not carry him into 1 Corinthians 15 unflagged.

### Early Adventist Voices

***Spiritual Gifts***, p. 16 (EGW Writings library, refcode SGL). This pioneer-era Adventist treatment guards against a real misreading of chapter 13:

> "The more excellent way is not to have a church without the gifts of the Spirit; it is a more excellent way than simply to 'covet' gifts."

The author's point is that the discourse on love does not dispense with the gifts. Reading 1 Corinthians 13:8–10 with 13:12, he concludes that the gift of prophecy may be manifest, as it pleases the Lord, until the perfect state comes — when the Lord is seen face to face, prophecy will no longer be needed. This is the classic Adventist exegetical answer to cessationism, and it is drawn from the text rather than asserted over it. *Note: refcode SGL; verify author attribution in the library record before naming a person from the pulpit.*

### Modern Evangelical Scholarship

Nothing in this section is a paraphrase of these commentaries. Two pointers:

- **Gordon Fee, *The First Epistle to the Corinthians* (NICNT)** is the standard technical commentary on these chapters and is cited approvingly in the Adventist volume above at exactly the points that matter (the meaning of "greater gift," why prophecy edifies). No claim is made here about its own conclusions beyond what Timm and Esmond quote.
- **Keener's IVP Bible Background Commentary** is where to verify the cymbal-and-gong cultic background claim in Section 2.

**On Barclay:** his Greek word backgrounds on agapē are widely circulated and often vivid. Use for cultural and linguistic color only. He holds positions on universal salvation and the resurrection that are not Adventist, and none of his theological conclusions should be cited as support for a point in this study.

### Bottom Line

The consensus across every accessible source is that 1 Corinthians 12–14 is a single pastoral argument against spiritual status-seeking, and that chapter 13 is its center rather than an interruption. SDABC, Matthew Henry, and Timm and Esmond independently converge on the same test: a gift's worth is measured by whether love is running through it and whether anything is being built up, not by how impressive it looks. The genuine tension is over the *nature* of tongues, where Adventist scholarship leans firmly toward real foreign languages on the strength of Acts 2 — a reading that makes Paul's demand for interpretation coherent — and where SDABC deliberately reserves its fuller argument for an Additional Note this study could not access. What a teacher most needs to know: the KJV's "charity" and its supplied "unknown" are the two translation decisions most likely to derail the class, so name them both early, and let 14:3 and 14:12 supply Paul's own definitions rather than importing modern ones.

## 5. Ellen G. White Cross-References

### 5.1. On loveless gifts, before the famous verses

> "No matter how high the profession, he whose heart is not filled with love for God and his fellow men is not a true disciple of Christ. Though he should possess great faith and have power even to work miracles, yet without love his faith would be worthless. He might display great liberality; but should he, from some other motive than genuine love, bestow all his goods to feed the poor, the act would not commend him to the favor of God. In his zeal he might even meet a martyr's death, yet if not actuated by love, he would be regarded by God as a deluded enthusiast or an ambitious hypocrite."

*The Acts of the Apostles*, p. 318.2 (para_id 127.1407) — **Tier 1, verbatim**

This is Ellen White walking through 1 Corinthians 13:1–3 in Paul's own order, and she does not soften the ending. "A deluded enthusiast or an ambitious hypocrite" is the strongest sentence in this study. It is also the pastorally dangerous one — read it as Paul's warning to a proud church, not as license for a class to start diagnosing each other.

### 5.2. On the character of love

> "Charity 'doth not behave itself unseemly, seeketh not her own, is not easily provoked, thinketh no evil.' Christ-like love places the most favorable construction on the motives and acts of others. It does not needlessly expose their faults; it does not listen eagerly to unfavorable reports, but seeks rather to bring to mind the good qualities of others."

*The Acts of the Apostles*, p. 319.2 (para_id 127.1411) — **Tier 1, verbatim**

"Places the most favorable construction on the motives and acts of others" is the most usable definition of 13:5–7 in Adventist literature. It converts an abstraction into a decision a member can make on the way home from church.

### 5.3. On why love outlasts every gift

> "Love 'rejoiceth not in iniquity, but rejoiceth in the truth; beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things.' This love 'never faileth.' It can never lose its value; it is a heavenly attribute. As a precious treasure, it will be carried by its possessor through the portals of the city of God."

*The Acts of the Apostles*, p. 319.3 (para_id 127.1412) — **Tier 1, verbatim**

Pairs directly with SDABC on 13:13. The gifts are scaffolding for this age; love is the one thing that goes through the gate.

### 5.4. On mutual dependence in the body

> "The bonds of unity which unite member with member of the church are to be as firm and harmonious in their operation as are the different parts of the natural body. The hands, head, and feet are so closely united, and so mutually dependent, that one member cannot live and act independently of the other members. The life and Spirit of Christ sustains and causes to flourish every particular member of his own body, and they live because Christ lives in them."

*Manuscript 44, 1900*, par. 35 (para_id 14065.9349066) — **Tier 1, verbatim**

She adds what Paul's anatomy illustration alone does not say: the body is not merely interdependent, it is alive *because Christ lives in it.* The unity is Christological, not organizational.

### 5.5. On not taking over another member's assignment

> "Each has a different work. No one is required to lay hold of another's work, and, though untrained, try to do it. God has given to each according to his ability. One man may think that his position gives him authority to dictate to other workers, but this is not so."

*Christian Leadership*, p. 38.2 (para_id 12.212), compiled from *Testimonies for the Church*, vol. 8, pp. 170–171 — **Tier 1, verbatim**

This is the practical edge for church officers, and it cuts a direction the passage's usual applications miss. 1 Corinthians 12 is normally preached at the member who feels useless. Ellen White aims the same principle at the member who feels entitled to run everybody else's department.

## 7. Theological Themes

### 7.1. Grace, not achievement

**In the text.** The gift vocabulary is *charismata*, built on *charis* (12:4). The Spirit divides "to every man severally as he will" (12:11). Even the foundational confession, "Jesus is the Lord," is possible only "by the Holy Ghost" (12:3). Every link in the chain is God's initiative.

**Adventist lens.** This is the same grace that saves, applied to service. Adventist soteriology insists sanctification flows out of justification and never the reverse, and 1 Corinthians 12 is that order in the realm of church work. A gift is not a reward for spiritual attainment. SDABC draws the practical conclusion: knowing all gifts come from God should forbid any display of pride in having them (6SDABC on 12:11).

**Pastoral implication.** The member who says "I don't have any gift" and the member who says "look at mine" are both misreading the same word.

### 7.2. Unity that requires diversity

**In the text.** 12:12–27. The foot that resents not being a hand and the eye that dismisses the hand are both destroying the body. Paul's inversion in 12:22–24 — the seemingly feeble parts are necessary, and God gives greater honor to the parts that lacked it — is aimed squarely at a status culture.

**Adventist lens.** No specific distinctive is engaged here; this is general Christian ecclesiology. But it lands hard in a denomination organized around departments, committees, and offices. Ellen White's warning against dictating to other workers (ChL 38.2) is this text applied to Adventist administration.

**Pastoral implication.** Uniformity is not the goal and never was. A church where everyone had the same gift would be, in the video's memorable phrase, a giant disembodied tongue.

### 7.3. Love as the criterion, not one gift among many

**In the text.** 12:31b–13:13. Paul does not rank love highest on the gift list. He removes it from the list and makes it the thing without which every item on the list equals zero. Note the arithmetic of 13:1–3: not *less*, but *nothing*.

**Adventist lens.** SDABC grounds love's permanence in the law: love is the foundation of the law, and God's law is eternal (6SDABC on 13:8). That is a characteristically Adventist move, and it links the love chapter to the great-controversy theme, where the issue between Christ and Satan is finally about the character of God's government.

**Pastoral implication.** The test of your ministry is not its size or its visibility. It is whether love is running through it.

### 7.4. Edification as the test for public worship

**In the text.** Chapter 14 turns on *oikodomē*. Prophecy outranks uninterpreted tongues in this setting for one stated reason: it is intelligible, so it builds (14:3–5, 12, 19). Five understood words beat ten thousand that nobody follows. And 14:40: let all be done decently and *kata taxin*, in rank and order.

**Adventist lens.** This is the biblical charter for reverent, intelligible, orderly Adventist worship, and SDABC's cross-references at 14:40 point straight into the Adventist literature on reverence in the house of God. It also supplies the Adventist critique of ecstatic worship: not that the Spirit's gifts are unwelcome, but that a gift which leaves the room confused has missed its own purpose.

**Pastoral implication.** The right question about anything done from the platform is not "was that powerful?" but "did the congregation understand it, and was anyone built up?"

### 7.5. The prophetic gift continues, always under Scripture

**In the text.** Paul ranks prophecy high (12:28; 14:1, 39) and defines it functionally in 14:3 as building, exhorting, comforting. He assumes the gift is operating and needs governing (14:29–32), not that it is about to expire.

**Adventist lens.** This is where the passage touches a distinctive directly. Adventists read Ephesians 4:11–13 as keeping the gifts in force until the church reaches full maturity in Christ, which has not happened; and Revelation 12:17 with 19:10 as identifying the prophetic gift as a mark of God's final faithful people. The pioneer treatment in SGL 16.2 anchors this in 1 Corinthians 13:8–12: prophecy ceases when we see face to face, not when the canon closes. Two guardrails belong with the claim. First, the tests of a prophet in Deuteronomy 13 and 18, Isaiah 8:20, Matthew 7:16, and 1 John 4:1–3 are meant to be *used*. Second, the gift is always subordinate to Scripture — the lesser light pointing to the greater. A genuine prophetic gift points back to the Bible and submits to be measured by it.

**Pastoral implication.** Neither of the easy positions is available to us. We do not dismiss prophetic claims, and we do not accept them uncritically. We test them by a Bible that outranks them.

### 7.6. The love chapter as a portrait of Christ

**In the text.** Read 13:4–8 and put the name of Jesus where the word love stands. It fits line by line: patient with disciples who kept misunderstanding Him; keeping no record of wrongs, from the cross, over the people killing Him; not insisting on His own way, in Gethsemane; bearing all things, enduring all things, all the way through.

**Adventist lens.** The gifts are given for the building up of *the body of Christ* — His body, not ours. Christ-centeredness is not a devotional flourish added to this chapter; it is what makes the ethics possible. The chapter is not primarily a standard to measure up to. It is a description of the One who has already loved us this way.

**Pastoral implication.** At the foot of the cross the competition dies. A person who has been loved at that cost no longer needs to prove they matter, and that is what finally frees them to love.

## 8. Application Questions

1. **Observational.** Read 1 Corinthians 13:1–3 slowly. List exactly what Paul says a person could possess and still be "nothing." Which item on that list would most impress your congregation if someone had it?

2. **Interpretive.** Paul had already said in 12:31 that he would show them "a more excellent way." Why does he interrupt a discussion of spiritual gifts to describe love, instead of simply telling the Corinthians to stop arguing and share the microphone? What does the placement of chapter 13 argue that a direct rebuke could not?

3. **Interpretive.** In 14:5 prophecy is "greater" than tongues, yet 12:11 says the Spirit distributes as He wills and 12:22 says the feebler members are necessary. How do you hold those together without either creating a permanent ranking of gifts or emptying "greater" of meaning? Test your answer against Timm and Esmond's point that prophecy is greater here *because it is intelligible*, not greater in itself.

4. **Applicational.** Name your gift out loud — not the one you wish you had, and not the one you envy. What has the Spirit actually placed in you? If you cannot name one, ask two people in the group what they have seen in you, and write down what they say.

5. **Applicational.** Think of a specific person in your congregation whose service is hidden and unapplauded: the one who prays, sets up chairs, cleans up, visits, notices the overlooked. What will you do this week to give "more abundant honour" (12:23) to that person by name?

6. **Applicational, costlier.** Take the one item from Paul's list in 13:4–7 that you fail most often — envying, boasting, insisting on your own way, keeping score, being easily provoked. Name the person with whom you fail at it. What does repentance look like in that specific relationship before next Sabbath?

7. **Applicational, costlier.** Where has your own gift become mostly about you: about being noticed, being needed, being thought spiritual, or winning a competition nobody else knows you are running? Be concrete about the ministry and the motive.

8. **Applicational, for leaders.** Read Ellen White in *Christian Leadership*, p. 38 again: no one is required to lay hold of another's work. Which part of the church body have you been dictating to, or quietly taking over, that God assigned to someone else? What would handing it back cost you?
