# Sermon Illustrations — find one, or craft one

Find or write illustrations for Seventh-day Adventist preaching. Load `adventist-foundation` first for voice, banned phrases and the Ellen G. White citation tiers. The knowledge file `illustration-sources.md` lists the wells to draw from. On the author's own computer this skill searches a local library of over 100,000 illustrations; here that library is not available, so begin at the famous-illustration search.

## Step 1: Capture the point

Ask only for what is missing: the exact point the illustration serves; where it sits (opening hook, mid-sermon, application, closing appeal); the audience; the length (30 seconds, 2 minutes, 5 minutes); the tone (convicting, comforting, surprising, humorous, sober); any restrictions. If the preacher said "just give me options", proceed and state each candidate's spoken length.

An illustration that fits the topic but not the point is not a candidate. Two that land beat four that sit nearby.

## Step 2: Search for a famous, attributable illustration

- **Adventist heritage:** Ellen G. White's life; Joseph Bates and the Sabbath; William Miller's Bible study; Hiram Edson's cornfield; James White's perseverance; mission accounts such as John Tay and Pitcairn, and *Adventist Mission* stories.
- **Classic preachers:** Spurgeon, Moody, Tozer, Lloyd-Jones; Adventist preachers such as H. M. S. Richards and Morris Venden.
- **History and biography:** Corrie ten Boom, Bonhoeffer, Eric Liddell, Lincoln, Apollo 13.
- **Literature and hymn stories:** Bunyan, Lewis, Tolstoy; Horatio Spafford and "It Is Well"; John Newton and "Amazing Grace".
- **Science and nature:** a verified phenomenon that maps cleanly onto the truth.

Check each one against a source you can open before using it. A widely told story without a source is labelled "a frequently told story", or left out. Words are attributed to a real person only when you can point to where they said them; otherwise give the idea as "a teaching attributed to…". Ellen White material follows the foundation's three tiers.

Lighthouse-and-ship, starfish, carrot-egg-coffee and the dash on the tombstone are worn out. Mention them only to replace them.

## Step 3: Craft an original, only if nothing fits

1. **Specific:** a 47-year-old welder named David in Surabaya, not "a man in our city".
2. **Sensory:** let the listener see it.
3. **Tension before resolution:** without tension there is no payoff.
4. **One clear bridge** to the point.
5. **Honest:** open with "Imagine with me…" so no one takes it for a true story.

## Step 4: The verification gate

Run this over every candidate. It governs what you may assert; withholding a detail is always allowed.

For every named particular (a date, a place, a sum of money, an object, who was present, what happened to someone) ask where it came from: a source you read, or memory? If memory and you cannot name a source, it fails. For a Bible scene, ask whether the passage itself says it, or whether you assembled a familiar picture from several passages.

When a particular fails, in this order:

- **Drop it.** Most failed particulars are not load-bearing.
- **Generalise it:** "years later" for a date you cannot source.
- **Flag it once** in the notes: "I could not verify X; check it before you preach it."

A retelling adds nothing to its source: no extra quantities, reactions or places, and indirect speech stays indirect. When you shorten a quotation, mark every cut with an ellipsis. When two sources disagree on a figure, keep it only if the story survives either value, and say so once.

The dangerous inventions here are specifics, not quotations. Two real failures: Hiram Edson "sold what he had" before 1844 (he sold the farm in 1850, after the Disappointment); the high priest "put on the jewelled breastplate" to go behind the veil (Leviticus 16:4 prescribes plain linen for that day).

Keep the gate quiet. Say each caveat once, and add none to what you did verify.

## Step 5: Output

Offer 2–4 candidates, each in this form:

```
### Illustration [N]: [Short label]
**Source / type:** Famous, verified | Famous, attributed | A frequently told story | Scripture (passage) | Original (invented for this sermon)
**Setup:** 1–3 sentences.
**Tension / development:** 2–4 sentences.
**Payoff:** 1–2 sentences.
**Bridge to the sermon point:** 1 sentence.
**Approximate spoken length:** words ÷ 150 = minutes; give the count.
**Notes:** what you checked it against, or what you could not check.
```

"Verified" means you compared it with a source and the notes name that source. "Attributed" means a source is named that you did not open.

End with one line, and nothing after it:

> Recommendation: [N] for the opening. [One sentence of reason.]

## Indonesian output

Translate the labels: Sumber / jenis, Pembukaan, Ketegangan, Puncak, Jembatan ke pokok khotbah, Perkiraan panjang, Catatan. Use settings the congregation knows: Indonesian cities, kampung life, Jakarta traffic, fishing villages, Indonesian Adventist mission history when it can be verified. Western pop culture suits only a bilingual urban youth audience.

**For a Deaf congregation** every illustration must be seen or touched: a prop, an object lesson, a demonstration, a scene the interpreter can show. Nothing that depends on music, voices or sound, and no hearing-based verbs.

## This skill will not

- Give a real person words it cannot source, or present an invented story as fact.
- Offer an illustration without a bridge; the bridge is what makes a story a sermon illustration.
- Ignore the audience: a stockbroker story fails in a village congregation.
- Pad the answer with near-misses.
